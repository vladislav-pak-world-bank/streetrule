    Three ways a rule lookup goes wrong

    Most tools match a city name to a list of laws. Each of these mistakes changes the answer for real buildings in the sample.

    The mailing city is not the legal city

    Dorchester, Roxbury and Allston are Boston. San Ysidro is San Diego. A city-name match sends their tenants to the wrong law.

    Coverage hides in the fine print

    San Francisco rent control stops at buildings first certified after June 13, 1979. California's cap exempts buildings under 15 years old. StreetRule reads those thresholds and checks them.

    A bill is not a law, and dates disagree

    New Jersey's FAIR Act is enacted but starts in 2027. Massachusetts's algorithm bills have not passed. Berkeley's ban has two published start dates.

    Unknown is a to-do list

    Public records leave out facts that decide coverage. Across the 500 addresses, these are the missing facts behind every "unknown", ranked by how many answers each one would settle.

    A property manager already knows these facts. On any address, enter them in the facts panel and the answer is recomputed on the spot, with each check shown. The public-data version tells you what to collect; the manager's own data closes it.

    How it works

    Every step is automated, inspectable, and tested. Read the method and the evidence.

    Read the law

    A model reads each of the supplied documents and returns rules in a strict schema. A rule is kept only if its quote appears word for word in the source.

    Extract the thresholds

    A second pass turns coverage text into numbers: cutoff dates, unit limits, owner-occupied and new-construction exemptions.

    Find the legal city

    Each address goes to the US Census Geocoder. A match to a different house number is rejected rather than trusted.

    Decide, and show the work

    Plain arithmetic decides each rule as of the query date: applies, unknown, superseded, not yet effective, or pending. Every check is listed.
